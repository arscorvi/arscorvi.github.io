#### PREFACE
<div class="notes">
This review was originally written on January 15th, 2025. I'm not particularly happy with it, as is so often the case with my writing, and I fully intend to rewrite it in the future. Nevertheless, the original text is being kept here for archival purposes.
</div>

I first encountered the work of Karl Ove Knausgård whilst I was browsing the shelves at my local bookshop; there, a lone copy of the final entry in his *'My Struggle'* series, aptly named *'The End'*, caught my eye, and after scanning the book's pages for only a moment, I made a mental note, went home, and promptly ordered the entirety of the series.

Normally, I tend to disregard descriptors like 'The International Phenomenon' that sits atop all of the covers of the prints by Vintage as hyperbole, but as I came towards the end of *'A Death in the Family'*, I am now confident that the notion of this book being a 'phenomenon' is perhaps one of the only words that can come to describe it. Memoir, biography, fiction, or all three at once, it can be hard to tell. Certainly, there are aspects of Knausgård's writing that beggar belief — who remembers, for example, the exact details of conversations held five, ten, fifteen years ago? — but this is something that, perhaps surprisingly, is not a detriment at all; rather, it is in the candid recollection of events that enabled me, as a reader, to pull myself along the plotted out threads of the life being told and come to know these moments and places and people almost as familiarly as I know my own.

Perhaps this ease came to me solely because there was an awful lot that I could relate to. The whimsical and almost picturesque childhood, the emotional absence of Knausgård's father, the desire to write, to be better than circumstance would allow, to make *something* of ourselves and how, in the face of death and loss and grief, all of those things tend to wash away, become complicated bundles of memories, wants, desires, fading into obscurity in the face of something as monumental as death. Then, a realization; that death in and of itself is mundane, that there is no complication there, that it is simply an immutable fact. It takes an awful lot of words to get to the point, but the point is made nonetheless, and I am grateful for it.

I don't believe it would be fair to give this book any less than five stars. Not because it is, standalone, an impeccable piece of literature — there are certainly parts that drag, paragraphs that run for just a little too long, sentences that are strung together haphazardly and given no further thought — but because of what it evokes, the reflection it brings, and the strange, comforting feeling that is born from viewing another's life through their own lens and, in so doing, coming face to face with ourselves.
