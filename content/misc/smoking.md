I never thought that I would grow up to be a smoker. The broad, sweeping landscape of memories from my childhood — so alien to me, now — cherry-picks recollections of days spent without rhyme, reason, or structure; of my brother, my mother, or my father at random; and feeds them to me with an unsteady, coltish hand. By and large, these memories are pleasant ones and not at all unwelcome, with each and every one of them coloured, naturally, by my perception of these events, but also by more fluid concepts and hazy meanings attached to the people in them; in the case of my father, for example, my view of him is dictated by a core set of strangely complimentary tones: the elusive, seemingly forever out-of-reach pedestal of masculinity, safety, and guidance that comes with being a father; my own desire, particularly as I grew, to climb up alongside him atop that peculiar throne to stand, side-by-side, as equals; and the cloying smell of second-hand smoke that has come to suffuse each and every memory with an offensive odour that blurs his figure and shrouds him in a thick, enigmatic haze.

I don't intend to write at length about my father, nor my childhood, but I cannot simply ignore them in the context of this page; they are both, in equal measure, a core foundation of the path that led me towards smoking; romanticism on one hand and idolisation on the other. Memories of my father igniting his cigarettes — bringing out his lighter, a dirty and well-worn Venetian Brass Zippo, and effortlessly flicking it open, striking his thumb, and producing a mesmerising, for-adults-only flame with confidence and practised ease — define the former in this case, whilst the latter only begins to form and come together in the thickness of that second-hand smoke, amidst the harsh scent, the billowing clouds, and the dusting of ash.

How on Earth did I ever come to pick up a cigarette myself? I didn't always idolise the act of smoking; if you had asked me, when I was in my mid-teens, what the singular most disgusting and destructive habit a person could develop was, my answer would have been easy, quick, and confident: smoking. The concept baffled me to no end, not least of all because of the health risks; everybody *knew* that smoking was bad for you, everybody *knew* that they were hurting themselves, but they were still doing it, they were still smoking and would continue to smoke, burning through stick after stick with a learned disregard for their health. It defied all sense of reason. I couldn't understand it. And even though I afforded my father no small amount of excuses for being blind to it all — he had smoked from an exceptionally early age and had grown up in a culture that was dominated by smoking — I still longed, in spite of my own idolisation, for the day that he would quit.

I could easily chalk it up to naivety, the juvenile idea that I would never become like them, like those *smokers*, blind as I myself was to the myriad of reasons as to why substances such as nicotine are so popular. I could just as easily dismiss it as something that was, ultimately, out of my control; parental smoking in childhood is a well-documented pathway to smoking initiation, and could thus absolve me of my failings and lay the blame elsewhere, somewhere out of sight and out of mind. But I am not so quick to shirk responsibility and dodge blame; I became a smoker because I wanted to smoke. It was a decision — a quick one, without *too* much thought behind it — that I made.

But how did I come to make that decision? When did I make it? *Why* did I make it?

## Romanticism and Idolisation

I'm afforded only brief snippets of thoughts and memories as to how this habit formed. Chief amongst them is that aforementioned romanticism, something that grew and developed in a wildly uncontrolled fashion in my mid-to-late teenage years, influenced heavily by works such as David Levithan's [The Realm of Possibility](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/23232). There, a poem opens with the following lines:

> *i've never smoked a cigarette \
> with anyone but jed. \
> senior year, driver's licenses, \
> our town is so many miles \
> with nowhere to go. \
> nowhere but the woods, \
> where leaves block out the haze of the city \
> blocking out the stars. \
> we pass the cigarette hand to hand, and \
> somehow i can see the trail of smoke in the \
> darkness. the way i can see jed's eyes \
> even when there isn't any light.*

This was a poem that captivated me from start to finish; it shifted my perception of smoking from something that was unpleasant and dangerous to something that was attractive, secretive, romantic, and altogether enticing. I was sixteen-years-old. My identity was effortlessly pliable, shaped in the abstract palms of books, music, films, and poetry. I didn't have to *be* myself, as I had been all of those years of my life up until that point; if I wanted to, if I really, really wanted to, I could be like the boys in the poem. I could be like Daniel. I could be like Jed.

And, oh, how I wanted to be like them! Is it at all surprising that, by the time I bought my first cigarette packet at nineteen, I was still full of these romantic ideals? That I would come to impose rules and restrictions upon myself — one cigarette per week, no more, no less — that were dictated by a later passage in that same damned poem?

> *i am willing to smoke the cigarette until \
> it disappears. jed tells me when it's time to stop. \
> i reach into the pack for another but jed \
> says one is enough. anyone can do more, \
> but it will be our thing to do just one.*

I don't believe so. Nor is it surprising, either, that I failed to stick to that oddly specific rule for very long; as addictive and uplifting as nicotine is, it took little to no time at all for the boundaries to begin to blur and for things to change before I could even realise it. One cigarette a week became two, two became three, and, soon enough, that idyllic restriction had well and truly flown the nest. One cigarette a day. Two cigarettes a day. Three, four, five.

It would be unfair, though, to say that my habit was formed entirely by the influence of David Levithan's poetry, just as it would be unfair to lay any blame upon my father; whilst my romanticism and idolisation of smoking stems almost exclusively from these factors, their abstract nature comes together to form only one of the two more easily definable reasons. The other reason — the more elusive one, the one that hides in dim corners and whimpers, cries, begs not to be noticed or addressed — is intense, all-consuming depression.

## Depression and Self-Harm

My memories of the years in which I began to smoke are a veritable mess; their landscape is jagged, rising and falling without any discernible pattern, a confusing mixture of clearer, lucid moments that are hidden haphazardly amidst murky, impossible to recognise events, places, and people. These were the years wherein I was first diagnosed with depression after failing to convince my family — and myself, to a degree — that everything was fine, that I was okay, that I had it all under control, and the frequency with which I was smoking was a firm indicator of just how troubled, anxious, and frightened I was; they were a comforting, easily reached for companion during moments of stress. They were a means, in no small amount of ways, to shield myself from what was happening around me. It didn't matter how the day had started, what had happened, nor what was *going* to happen; I could always sneak myself out onto our front porch, light a cigarette, and the world itself would oh-so-kindly grind to a halt and wait, patiently and with great care, for me to drag it down to the filter and discard it. I was able to put myself in a bubble, I was able to stop my worrying, and I was able to live without the burden of that overbearing and ever-present weight of anxiety for the fleeting few minutes it took me to smoke a single cigarette from start to finish.

It didn't really *fix* anything, but it did make me feel better, if only for a moment, and that particularly debilitating period of depression continued, unchallenged, for a handful of years afterwards. My habitual smoking was thus afforded more than enough time for the act to become an addictive and stubborn pattern of behaviour that would prove exceedingly difficult to shake off, and to complicate matters even further, to all but assure I would not have been able to quit smoking any time soon, I began to look forward to and long for cigarettes in a much, much more dangerous light.

I've already mentioned that everybody knows that smoking is bad for you, that everybody knows that they're hurting themselves; I was not only keenly aware of this fact, but also strangely enticed by it, mesmerised and comforted — in a way that I still cannot quite understand — by the frustratingly self-destructive notion of doing something, *anything*, that could do me harm. People would parrot warnings, buzzwords, and clinically proven facts to me on a regular basis and I, in my desperation and cocky, self-assured bastardisation of wisdom, would see these things as *good*, as a kind of paradoxical salve for my struggles. I would smoke more, cruelly relishing the fact that I was harming myself, that I was causing myself *suffering*, even if I may not come to feel the effects of that suffering until many, many years into my future; unsurprisingly, I had little to no perception of a future to begin with, back then, and it quickly became a confusing kind of exercise in attempting to screw myself over without ever knowing if I would ultimately succeed.

## Smoking, Nowadays

I no longer smoke for any of the above reasons; though I undoubtedly considered my reasoning at the time to be sound, just, and without fault, that is no longer the case, and the act of smoking has taken on something of a life of its own. Now, it is nothing more than a comfort and a key part of my day-to-day routine; whatever thought processes went into the formation of my smoking as a habit have long since been lost to the blurry, ill-defined passage of time between then and now.

I naively told my friends and family that I would simply never allow myself to become addicted to smoking, that I was better than that, that cigarettes, with their harsh, acrid smell, would never come to be a defining character trait. I was wrong. Nicotine is addictive, and I am not an infallible beacon of virtue; I smoke *because* I am addicted. I may understand *why* I started smoking, I may have forgiven myself for the gleeful and childish ease with which I celebrated harming myself, but much like how I once longed for the day that my father would quit smoking, I now long for, and look forward to, the day that I am able to say that I quit.

I try my best to restrict myself to approximately 5 cigarettes a day, and I am strict with the manner in which I smoke; there are certain inalienable rules, ones that are much easier to keep than, for example, the one previously mentioned: I do not smoke around others without sheepishly asking permission before lighting up, and never around children; I do not smoke indoors or in communal spaces; I wash my hands and rinse my mouth after each cigarette. I try, no matter how impossible or awkward it is, to be as conscientious as possible when it comes to my smoking. It is my vice — a decision that I made, a habit that I personally formed, for this reason, that reason, for every reason in-between — and nobody else's.
